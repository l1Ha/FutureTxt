# Chapter 1: Borrowed Face

At 2:40 a.m., He Li’s phone buzzed: her mother had used He Li’s auto‑payment quota to buy twelve bottles of **Guli Sheng** in a live‑stream room.  

She groped her way upright, the screen stabbing her eyes. The order was followed by a line of fine print — **298 Yuan per bottle, scheduled delivery, auto‑deduction if not cancelled within 48 hours**. She tapped the link. The live‑stream showed 6,712 viewers, a backdrop of white coats and a silk banner, the host holding a spray bottle and telling the camera, “Back‑pain isn’t old age; it’s your bones leaking air.”  

The host wore He Li’s face.  

It was a face smoothed by algorithm. The brow ridge was shallower than hers, the jaw narrower, the smile frozen at the same angle, resetting to the initial pixels every fifteen seconds. Only the thin white scar beneath the left brow remained — the platform had kept it “for recognizability.” In the lower‑right corner of the product page a line of **Size‑6 Gray Text** read: “Contains AI‑generated content.” She brought the screen close before she could make out the words.  

The inventory counter ticked down: twelve, eight, five… When it hit three it snapped back to twelve. Her mother had snatched the deal in that flicker. He Li watched the number for a minute — it fell, then bounced back, like a claw machine that never lets you win.  

Her roommate on the upper bunk turned over. Twelve square metres, three beds, two night‑shift girls, a communal noodle pot. He Li threw on her coat, walked to the end of the corridor, sat beside the fire hydrant and called her mother. Seven rings.  

“Lili,” her mother’s voice was hoarse, “the medicine works. Old Wang and I each got six bottles.”  

“Mom, that live‑stream — ” He Li kept her voice flat. “Don’t buy any more.”  

“Why not? You’re the one selling it.”  

“I’m not selling it.”  

Her mother laughed. “Who’s bored enough to steal your face to peddle drugs?”  

The corridor’s motion‑sensor light flickered off, then on again. He Li stared at the hand resting on her knee. Eight months earlier, when she signed that contract, the Yunxiang authorization manager had said the exact same thing — “Relax, who’s bored enough to take your face and do something else with it?”  

She had once worked as a sales clerk at a mall women’s‑wear counter. Last autumn the mall switched to “generative seller‑shows”: a real model shoot cost 680 Yuan; an AI‑generated image cost 40. Her shift shrank from six hours to two, then to zero. The younger clerks were cleared out as well, leaving only the manager and a machine that could spit out pictures.  

The contract was shoved onto her phone then. **Yunxiang Image Authorization Platform**, exclusive authorization, five‑year term, 3,200 Yuan paid on the 3rd of each month. Eleven checkboxes on the page; the first ten she recognized — name, ID card, facial data, voiceprint collection, liveness‑verification backup. The eleventh read: “The platform may sub‑authorize the authorized image to ecosystem partners.” She paused three seconds on that line.  

In those three seconds she thought of her mother. The remote‑care package cost 2,800 Yuan a month; a caregiver visited Tuesdays and Fridays; the blood‑pressure cuff and pill box were online; a single day’s outage triggered a red‑alert from the county platform. She checked all eleven boxes.  

The first three months were ordinary. In month two the platform listed “He Li·Everyday Image,” a one‑off buy‑out netting her 960 Yuan. In month three her face appeared on the hero images of three down‑jacket shops — styles she had never worn. She asked customer service; they sent a *Scope of Authorization Use Statement*, a seventeen‑page PDF. Clause 3 on page 7 stated: “Sub‑authorization requires no further notice.”  

She saved the file and never opened it again.  

The care‑package fee was auto‑deducted from the authorization revenue account. Every 3rd the platform deposited 3,200 Yuan; the 4th it withdrew 2,800 Yuan, leaving 400 Yuan — her entire budget for food, transport, rent. Last month she was a day late on repayment; the caregiver waited ten minutes at the door then left. Her mother said on the phone, “It’s fine, Mom doesn’t blame you,” three times.  

Now her mother was counting bottles on the line: “Three sprays a day, just enough to last till New Year. Old Wang says after half a month his legs stopped hurting.”  

“Mom,” He Li said, “does that drug have a real registration number?”  

“What registration?” Her mother paused. “Listen.”  

Through the phone came a familiar drum‑beat. Her mother held the handset up to the TV — the village’s **Xiyanghong** big screen was hooked to the old set. On the screen, He Li’s face addressed a roomful of seniors: “Last thirty orders, act fast.”  

“See?” her mother said. “You look prettier smiling than you do at home.”  

At 3:06 a.m. He Li typed her first line into the platform’s complaint portal.  

The bot replied in 0.8 seconds, punctuation pre‑fabricated: “Hello, ticket K‑20261101‑0783 created. Please upload ① unauthorized‑use declaration ② biometric verification report from a qualified institution ③ authorization contract number. Processing 30‑45 business days. If sub‑authorization is involved, negotiate with the sub‑licensee first.”  

She read it twice; every character was familiar, yet the sentence felt alien.  

She asked: “The video shows my face — why not take it down first?”  

Bot: “Platform follows ‘notice‑and‑takedown’ rules. Until verification finishes, the platform cannot determine whether the image was authorized by you.”  

She asked: “How do I prove that face wasn’t authorized by me?”  

Bot: “Provide a biometric verification report.”  

She asked: “How many accounts has that face been authorized to?”  

Bot: “Please wait.”  

Half a minute later a list refreshed: **17 partner accounts** — down‑jackets, massage chairs, senior shoes, health wine, plus three live‑stream rooms she didn’t recognize. She scribbled the first four, filed infringement complaints one by one. On the fifth a gray line appeared: “Your ticket duplicates K‑20261101‑0783; merged for handling.”  

She folded the handwritten page, slipped it into her pocket. The hallway draft slipped through the fire‑door crack, rustling the plastic bag at her feet.  

At 4:30 a.m. she pulled up the contract and read it clause by clause.  

**Clause 7.3**: “The authorized image may be sub‑authorized to ecosystem partners; sub‑authorization revenue settled proportionally.”  

**Clause 9.1**: “The authorizer shall not make statements damaging the commercial reputation of the platform or its partners; violation entitles the platform to terminate the contract and claw back three times the paid earnings.”  

She calculated: the platform had paid 25,600 Yuan total; threefold = 76,800 Yuan. Her account held 384.60 Yuan.  

At 7:00 a.m. she investigated “Guli Sheng.” The product page displayed a blurry registration‑certificate photo, the number half‑covered by a watermark. She completed the number, searched the county government service portal — red text returned: “Registration revoked in 2124.” She screenshotted it and filed a report with the market‑supervision hotline. The final field read: “Upload proof of identity with the implicated image.”  

Identity proof required a biometric verification report. That required an appointment. The earliest slot was the 20th of next month, fee 480 Yuan.  

At 9:30 a.m. she went to the district Market Supervision Office.  

The hall was packed. The ticket machine spat **A107** — thirty‑four people ahead. When her turn came, the clerk flipped through her phone screenshots: “Revoked registration is a fact; we’ll note it. But handling the implicated image first requires confirming the infringed party.”  

“The face is mine,” He Li said.  

“The system shows 4,380 usage records for this face; the authorization chain sits with the platform.” The clerk pushed the phone back. “We need identity proof to list you as the aggrieved party. Otherwise we close as ‘unable to determine infringed party.’”  

“How long to close?”  

“30‑45 business days.”  

“That live‑stream is still selling.”  

“Yes. We can’t move that fast.”  

When He Li retrieved her phone the screen lit up. In the family chat her mother had forwarded a live‑stream link with three characters: “Support daughter.” Six relatives; four had already liked it.  

At 10:00 a.m. she returned to the apartment, trying to cancel the twelve‑bottle COD order.  

The order detail read: “Cancellable within 48 hours.” She tapped cancel — a prompt appeared: “To protect senior rights, cancellation of auto‑payment orders requires the purchaser to complete facial verification.” Her mother’s three‑year‑old phone had a fogged camera; even QR scans struggled.  

She called her mother, asked her to open the order and face the screen.  

“Aligned,” her mother said, holding the phone up. “Nothing shows.”  

“Mom, pull the phone back a bit, try again.”  

By the fourth try her mother laughed: “Too much trouble, forget it. I’m not returning them; they’re yours anyway.”  

He Li sat on the bed edge, staring at the order. Twelve bottles, 3,576 Yuan, auto‑deduction in 48 hours. She had 47 hours left.  

At 9:00 a.m. the Yunxiang authorization manager called.  

“Ms. He, I saw the ticket,” the voice was soft, as if afraid to wake someone. “This isn’t theft; the channel used the wrong asset. Sign a supplemental authorization and we’ll retroactively recognize the prior use. Thirty‑percent split, back‑paid to your account — everyone saves face.”  

“That drug is fake,” He Li said.  

“Whether it’s fake is for market supervision; you needn’t worry.” A pause. “If you keep complaining we’ll invoke Clause 9.1. The contract says sub‑authorization is allowed — you can’t disprove it.”  

“What if I refuse to sign?”  

“Then you’re the breaching party.” The manager said, “Ms. He, frankly, even if this video is taken down, another account reopens in fifteen minutes. Your effort isn’t worth it.”  

“Whose voice is in the video?” He Li asked.  

“Synthesized audio.” The manager said, “Voice isn’t in your authorization scope; we’ll handle it.”  

“How?”  

“Swap the voice.” A chuckle. “Your face works with any voice.”  

He Li didn’t reply. The line went dead first.  

Before noon she rode the metro back to the mall. The former women’s‑wear counter remained; the window displayed new model images. She stood outside the glass, noticing the right‑hand image’s model had **three arms** — the extra one resting on the hip, fingers pointing the wrong way.  

A former colleague emerged from the counter with a water cup, froze when she saw He Li.  

“That picture,” He Li pointed, “how much per image?”  

“Thirty‑eight,” the colleague said. “Manager says it saves six hundred plus versus before.”  

“Nobody notices the extra arm?”  

“Some do,” the colleague jerked her chin toward the review section. “They curse a bit then move on. It’s cheap.”  

The colleague switched the cup to the other hand. “Sis, what’ve you been up to lately?”  

He Li said, “I’m shooting images for a shop now.”  

At noon her mother called again.  

“Lili, the courier at the station said you have twelve COD parcels; I had them sent to your uncle’s place.” Her mother lowered her voice, as if sharing good news. “I saved them for you — you should spray some too, you stand all day.”  

“Mom,” He Li said, “listen — that medicine can’t be taken.”  

“Why not? Old Wang took it and it worked.”  

“Mom,” she looked at the faded family portrait on the wall, “the person selling that drug isn’t me.”  

Silence on the line. Her mother’s voice dropped. “Then who is it?”  

He Li didn’t answer. She didn’t know where to start: the 40‑Yuan AI model images, the eleven checkboxes, Clause 7.3, the 96,000 Yuan penalty, the Size‑6 Gray Text she only noticed yesterday in the hallway. She only heard her mother’s breathing mixed with the TV’s drumbeat, thudding far away.  

“Lili,” her mother said, “if you’re in trouble, tell Mom.”  

“No trouble,” He Li said. “Mom, I lent out my face.”  

“To whom?”  

“To a mouth,” she said. “It wears my face and speaks someone else’s words.”  

She hung up. The “supplemental authorization” link the manager sent still sat in her messages. She opened it.  

The page was clean: **Retroactive authorization amount 12,800 Yuan, split 30 %**, fine print: “Upon confirmation, deemed no objection to all uses.” The confirm button glowed green, far more inviting than the gray line on the complaint page. She stared at the green button a long time, then backed out and called the care platform.  

“Hello,” she said, “what happens if this month’s deduction fails?”  

“The system will suspend home‑visit service after 48 hours,” the operator said. “Restoration requires a new contract and the principal’s facial verification.”  

The word **“principal”** lingered in the receiver.  

That afternoon He Li went to the **Identity Verification Center** in the city’s south.  

Two queues filled the hall. Left: “Lost‑Record Re‑entry.” Right: “Biometric ID Revocation.” Only one revocation window, four people ahead.  

First, a twenty‑something woman clutching a stack of printouts, shoulders shaking with sobs. Second, a middle‑aged man repeatedly asking, “If I revoke, can my medical insurance still reimburse?” Third, a masked figure silent throughout. Fourth, when it was his turn, tore up his ticket and walked away.  

He Li became fifth, waited two hours.  

The verification officer leafed through her documents: “Confirm revocation? After revocation all verifications return ‘No Match’; no manual review; re‑enrollment queues to March next year.”  

“I understand.”  

“You’re being impersonated?”  

“Yes.”  

The officer glanced at her. “Last month a girl came to revoke because her former company’s access control blocked her. The day after revocation she couldn’t even book a hospital appointment.” He slid a paper notice across. “Sign here.”  

He Li picked up the pen. She hadn’t hand‑written her name in years. The characters “何丽” trembled on the paper; the final stroke of “丽” trailed a tiny tail, as if unfinished.  

The terminal began a countdown. A voice intoned each word: “Revocation will disable all facial verification services — finance, medical insurance, social security, access control, travel — irreversible. Confirm by pressing ‘Yes’.”  

She pressed her thumb.  

“Please speak the voiceprint passphrase.”  

She whispered the numeric string; her voice barely rose above the hall’s white noise.  

A green line appeared: “Revocation successful; all subsequent biometric verifications will return ‘No Match’.”  

She left the center as dawn broke.  

First stop: the bank. The teller swiped her ID card twice; the screen flashed a line. The teller glanced at the screen, then at He Li.  

“The ID proves who you are,” the teller said, “but the system must confirm you are the principal.”  

“I am the principal.”  

“I believe you.” The teller returned the card. “Manual verification channel is available, by appointment. The nearest slot is 17 March next year.”  

“My mother’s care fee,” He Li said, “is due today.”  

The teller didn’t answer immediately. The queue behind shuffled forward half a step.  

She left the bank, called the care platform again. This time she hammered “0” until a live agent answered. The reply matched the recording: restoration needs the principal’s facial verification, appointment queue, estimated four to six months.  

The subway gate’s red light stayed solid. She tapped her ID card — “Identity does not exist.” Another gate, same result. She pocketed the card and walked along the road. Rush‑hour crowds streamed past, each tapping the gate, green beeps chorusing.  

At the third intersection she paused at a convenience‑store entrance, wanting a bottle of water and a steamed bun. Shelves priced water at 2 Yuan. She patted her coat pocket — only the revocation receipt and a worn‑edge ID card. All her money lived in an account that opened only with her face.  

No cash drawer at the register, just a payment screen. She hadn’t seen a cash drawer in three years. She placed the water on the scanner, lifted her face to the screen.  

Red light: “Identity does not exist.”  

Behind the counter a wall‑mounted display looped the live‑stream. On it, He Li held “Guli Sheng,” telling the camera, “Last thirty orders, act fast.”  

The cashier glanced at the screen, then at He Li, blinked. “Aren’t you… the one selling the medicine?”  

He Li returned the water to the shelf, then the bun.  

“You’re mistaken,” she said.  

She pushed the door open. Behind her the electronic screen’s drumbeat kept accelerating, each beat sharper than the last.
