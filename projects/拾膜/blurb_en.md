When the Spring Thunder awakens the soil of Lunan Gazhuang, seventy-one-year-old Lao Ge unearths not just residual film, but nineteen years of evidence—nineteen glass jars on his windowsill, each sealing a season of white waste. His son, Ge Shengli, calculates input-output ratios, buying thinner "engineering film" to cover more acreage. Village supplier Lao Zhao weighs personal favors against fines, slipping banned film to regulars out back. Grandson Xiaojun, a packaging worker in Linyi, calculates factory deductions and travel costs. The new county technician calculates quotas and career prospects. Four generations tally accounts around this gossamer-thin plastic: profit, patronage, penalties, paychecks, performance metrics. Only Lao Ge, illiterate, knows a different arithmetic. He reads the white fog embedded in his fingernails, the debt that flies on the wind—forever uncollected, forever unpayable. A stark chronicle of intergenerational reckoning, where the "white revolution" that fed a village becomes the shackles on its land, and survival calculates its own ecological bankruptcy.
