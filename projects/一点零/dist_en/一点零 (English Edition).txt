The vision screening form came down that day. Xiao Zhou's right eye: zero point four.

The form was issued by the school, a sheet of thin paper, stamped in blue, with two lines of small print at the bottom: *Recommend re-examination at a regular medical institution.* Xiulan clutched the form in her hand, all the way from the supermarket's back door to the bus stop. She worked the register at Limin Supermarket, forty-two hundred a month. Month-end reconciliation meant standing all day, legs swollen. That day she'd rung up two transactions wrong, and the shift supervisor had said a couple of things. She hadn't made a sound.

Home. Xiao Zhou was doing homework. She stood in the doorway watching him: notebook inches from his face, elbow propped, head nodding lower and lower.

"Sit up straight."

"Oh." He pulled himself up, wrote for two minutes, then sank again.

She walked over, pulled the notebook out to look. Copied characters, neat rows, but the indentations were fresh — a classmate's handwriting. She flipped back a few pages. The earlier pages were a mess, lines running into each other, like they'd been rained on.

"Whose did you copy?"

"Wang Hao's."

"Can't you see it yourself?"

Xiao Zhou didn't speak. He capped his pen, uncapped it, capped it, uncapped it.

"How long?"

"Last year."

Xiulan stood there, still clutching the screening form. She remembered this past spring, him walking into a door frame once, her scolding him for being careless; remembered him taking books out to the balcony all summer, her saying the light was better there; remembered last month when he knocked over his water cup twice, her saying why was he always spacing out.

"Why didn't you say anything?"

Xiao Zhou's head stayed low, voice tiny: "Grandma's medicine costs so much."

Xiulan wore glasses herself. Twenty-five years. At fifteen, at the town middle school, she couldn't see the blackboard. She told her family. Her mother took her into town for a pair. On the way back, her mother said one sentence:

"Once you put glasses on, you never take them off. The prescription only gets deeper."

She'd remembered that sentence to this day. Later her prescription went from one-fifty to four hundred. She always felt her mother hadn't finished that sentence — couldn't take them off was true, deeper was true, but if she'd gotten them earlier, seen clearly sooner, maybe it wouldn't have come to this.

She didn't want Xiao Zhou walking her old road. But she was also afraid of glasses.

That night, she called Jianguo on the phone. Jianguo was on the highway, only called back from a service area, truck air brakes hissing in the background.

"How much?"

"Right eye zero point four. School says re-check."

"Get glasses then."

"Just get glasses?" she asked. "At that prescription, I hear once you put them on it's for life."

"What else can you do?"

"Let me see if there's another way."

"Don't go spending money recklessly." Jianguo said. "This month's car payment, Mom's medicine."

"I know."

She hung up and stood on the balcony for a while. Downstairs was the night market, barbecue gluten smoke drifting up. She turned off the balcony light, stood in the dark, thinking about the money in her card: mortgage twenty-three hundred, mother-in-law's meds eight hundred, car loan thirty-one hundred, Xiao Zhou's textbooks one-seventy. Her salary had hit yesterday, forty-two hundred.

The re-check was at City First Hospital, ophthalmology on the third floor. A row of kids in the corridor, all wearing trial frames.

Dilation drops went in. Xiao Zhou's eyes ached, light hurt, eyes closed against her shoulder. Forty minutes wait, number called.

Doctor Chen, forties, hair pulled back tight, spoke fast. She held Xiao Zhou's prescription, scanning column by column.

"Right eye one twenty-five, left eye one seventy-five, astigmatism fifty." She set the form down. "True myopia."

"Can it be reversed?"

"Can't be reversed." Doctor Chen said. "Axial length increased, doesn't shrink back. If we can control it, that's already good."

Xiulan's fingers tightened on her bag strap.

"Then what?"

"Three roads." Doctor Chen lifted her pen, wrote on paper. "One, single-vision lenses, three hundred or so, see the board without strain, prescription keeps climbing anyway. Two, defocus lenses, eight to twelve thousand a year, slows it a little. Three, ortho-k, fifteen thousand up, maintenance hassle, risks involved. You pick based on your family's situation."

"Which one can cure it?"

"None of them cure it." Doctor Chen said. "Myopia is a slope going forward. We can only brake, not reverse. Anyone who says it can be reversed is just after that money."

Xiulan left the exam room holding the form. In the corridor, a grandmother was pulling a doctor: "Would Chinese medicine work?" The doctor repeated: "No." The grandmother didn't leave. The doctor said it again, voice louder. The grandmother retreated to the wall, still muttering.

Xiao Zhou wore the little light-blocking glasses she'd given him, leaned against a chair, looking at her through the lenses. He couldn't see too clearly.

"Mom," he said. "Just get glasses. The three-hundred kind."

"Don't you worry about it."

"I can copy notes fine."

"Don't you worry about it."

On the bus home, Xiao Zhou fell asleep, head bobbing. Xiulan took out her phone, opened short video. She knew she shouldn't watch. She watched anyway. Searched three characters: *myopia can*.

Fifth video. A woman by a window, saying her kid was three hundred-plus degrees last year, now seventy-something, no classes, no glasses, just one patch one exercise.

Eight hundred-plus comments below. First one: *Please share contact.*

Xiulan watched once, then again. She saved the video.

Crossing the bridge, the river black. Xiao Zhou's head rested on her arm, breathing light. She looked down at his eyes, closed, a speck of light on his lashes.
