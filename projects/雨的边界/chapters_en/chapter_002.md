# Chapter 2: The Condensation Water Ledger of the Dry‑Zone Village

Night wind funneled in from the valley mouth, salty with brine and laced with the monotonous tap of the Condensation Towers—like someone fingernail‑tapping a row of empty tin cans, each strike blooming into a fine echo inside the mist. Lin Tan locked her exoskeleton’s micro‑motion pattern to low‑frequency; the analgesic patch on the inner knee softened under body heat but still leaked a dull ache, reminding her that every crossing of the rain‑line was paid for with bone. Her fingers brushed the worn magnetic tape in the side pocket of her pack, copper wires exposed at the frayed edge; the faint ridges of the data grooves were palpable, the sole physical proof of the 0.2 mm upward correction she had made a decade ago on her own authority. The wind also carried a rusted iron tang—the scent of abandoned orbital sleepers leaching iron in the damp night, climbing the nostrils straight to the throat so that every breath tasted of cold metal.  

The Dry‑Zone Village lay fifteen kilometres south of the rain‑line, a cluster of low, repurposed shipping containers that looked like forgotten building blocks under the stars. Thin‑film photovoltaics covered the roofs; nocturnal radiative cooling forced atmospheric vapor into minute beads that slid down the Condensation Towers’ guide channels into storage bladders. Lin Tan slipped through the narrow lane into the village core, boots on packed saline‑alkali soil that whispered “shh‑shh” with each step. First came the dry quartz dust, then the soggy rot of moss—soil and air on opposite sides of the rain‑line flipping completely within a single stride. Faded graffiti mottled the walls; a few lines of coordinate numbers had been sand‑blasted into illegibility, only the latest version tag still barely readable.  

Aya stood beside a Condensation Tower, clutching a yellowed slip of paper, fingertips dusted with moldy fibers. She was the village’s de facto leader, the only one who could read the handwritten “Rain‑line Coordinate / Baseline Version.” Seeing Lin Tan approach, Aya said nothing, merely handed over the note. On the back, faded ink read: **V‑3.1 165 mm**. Lin Tan took it, fingertips grazing the coarse surface; her pulse synced with the Biometric Fingerprint Pen’s sensor, and the display still showed **112.3 mm ± 0.12**. She recited quietly: “Reading 112.3 mm, error 0.12, within rain‑line spec.” A faint click sounded from her knuckles, followed by her mantra: “Millimetres don’t lie.” The worn edge of the slip still bore a smear of pale yellow grease—residue from the last time Aya lubed the tower’s bearings with scrap grease; a touch revealed the thin, slick film.  

Villagers formed a half‑circle, eyes devoid of anger, only the numbness of lives locked inside numbers. Every doorway displayed a travel credential, a blue watermark glowing faintly in the night: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Remaining 312 mm.” Lin Tan transferred 0.5 mm from her Programmable Quota Card into Aya’s palm, fingertips brushing the cold plastic rim; a sour guilt rose—she knew the amount was merely a rounding tail in the ledger, yet enough for a child to swallow one more mouthful of synthetic protein paste. The Insurance Consortium’s remote audit probes blinked atop the towers, a red sweep every few seconds confirming each droplet entered the Carbon‑Meter Dual‑Unit Ledger. The probes’ high‑frequency whine sounded like mosquitoes circling ears; villagers instinctively hunched, yet none dared protest.  

Aya murmured, “The Archive still holds magnetic tape; Curator Guan guards the original signed footage.” Before the words settled, a distant Maglev Pilgrimage Bus honked, a low electric hum cutting through the mist. Its side panels flickered: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp Swallowed by Rising Seas.” The bus rolled one‑way into the Abandoned Zone; inside, rust and damp mould mingled, while outside skeletal high‑rises pierced the sky like ribs. Lin Tan watched the taillights dissolve into the rain‑line’s shadow, traced a “∟” gesture on her screen—knuckles clicking once—then changed it to “✓,” shifting the semantics from “confirm” to “witness.” The cursor trembled in the night, as if answering an internal vow.  

She slipped the tape back into her pack; the zipper’s teeth meshed with a crisp snap in the silence. Knee pain spiked again, a reminder that the next leg would cross more invisible lines. Far off, an Orbital Scavenger shift wrestled 38 °C heat inside a Scavenger‑7 Operations Pod; coolant alarms flashed red, fragment‑capture quotas determining next year’s Cold Slot priority. Lin Tan regulated her breathing, engaged the exoskeleton, and stepped toward Gate 7’s Cold Slot entry. The saline‑alkali soil crunched under the exoskeleton’s micro‑vibrations, a fine brittle sound—as if the earth were quietly tallying the cost of each step.  

The metal grille at the Cold Slot entrance shuddered in the night breeze; the air tasted of ozone and stale grease, a metallic bitterness on the tongue. Exoskeleton joints hissed hydraulically; each footfall compacted the soil into fine powder, a soft “click‑clack” reverberating against her eardrums. Lin Tan lifted her gaze; a holographic panel projected a pale‑blue quota ledger into the fog: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Used 888 mm, Remaining 312 mm,” numbers pulsing under the red scan like a heartbeat. She instinctively pinched her fingertips; knuckles clicked again; she whispered, “Numbers won’t lie to me.”  

“Credentials.” A rasping electronic voice emanated from the turnstile, its sound refracting in the metal conduit into a piercing shriek. Lin Tan tapped her Programmable Quota Card to the reader; the blue watermark flared, the 0.5 mm tail deducted and instantly written to the ledger; the screen flashed “Confirmed ✓.” A chill of plastic brushed her fingertips; the same sour guilt surged—this fraction was a rounding artifact in the ledger, yet it could buy Aya another sip of synthetic protein paste.  

“Don’t rush, a Maglev Checkpoint lies ahead.” A low male voice came from behind, scented with machine oil. Lin Tan turned to see a scavenger in a worn radiation‑shield coat, a still‑smoking coolant tube in hand, face etched with wrinkles from chronic heat. He offered the tube: “Take it; keep the coolant alarm from locking you inside.”  

Lin Tan accepted; fingertips met the tube’s faintly scorching metal, brow furrowing then smoothing; knuckles clicked once more. She slotted the tube into the exoskeleton’s heat‑sink; the hydraulic pump’s whine steadied, red light died, green illuminated. The maglev track beneath thrummed with a low‑frequency pulse, a giant heart propelling her forward through the gate, into the precisely calculated Cold Slot where every metre was logged as a “Carbon‑Meter” expense. The pilgrimage bus’s taillights had long vanished into the rain‑line; only a faint blue glow at the track’s end lingered, trembling in the night wind—an oath awaiting the next soul who would count millimetres into bone and blood.  

The Maglev Checkpoint’s gate glowed ghost‑blue in the mist; a scanner’s red line swept over Lin Tan’s exoskeleton, emitting a short “zzt—” before the screen read “Carbon‑Meter 0.12 mm, Quota Synced.” She felt the invisible magnetic cradle support her spine; each pulse pressed the deep knee ache more firmly. The scavenger re‑clipped the coolant tube to his belt, fingertips sparking faintly on the metal latch, wiping oil‑sweat from his cheek.  

“Quota enough to last till next season?” he asked, voice reverberating in the conduit, laced with oil and ozone. Lin Tan didn’t look up; another knuckle click, the numbers flashing before her: “Remaining 311.5 mm, just enough to get Aya into the next synthetic protein tier.” She tapped the card to the secondary reader; blue light flared, 0.3 mm deducted, the ledger gaining a line “Witness ✓.”  

Ahead, the Cold Slot curved into darkness, its surface glazed with thin frost; the magnetic field compressed the saline soil into fine sand, each step a soft “shh‑shh,” as if the ground were quietly bookkeeping. Far off, the Maglev Pilgrimage Bus honked again; its advertisement flickered in the night: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp Swallowed by Rising Seas.” Taillights were swallowed by rain; only the faint blue glow at the track’s terminus pulsed, waiting for the next traveler who would etch millimetres into marrow.
