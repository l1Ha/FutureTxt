# Chapter 2: The Condensation Water Ledger of the Dry‑Zone Village

Night wind funneled in from the mouth of the valley, carrying a briny salt‑frost taste mixed with the monotonous tapping of the condensation towers—like fingernails rapping a row of empty tin cans, each strike blooming into a fine echo in the thin mist. Lin Tan locked her exoskeleton’s micro‑motion mode to low frequency; the analgesic patch on the inner side of her knee softened slightly under body heat, yet a dull ache persisted, reminding her that every crossing of the Rainline was a debt paid in bone. She felt the worn magnetic tape in her pack’s side pocket, its frayed edges exposing copper wire; her fingertips traced the faint ridges of the data grooves, the sole physical evidence of the 0.2 mm upward correction she had made a decade ago. The wind also bore a hint of rusted iron, the scent of abandoned orbital sleepers leaching rust in the damp night, crawling up the nostrils and settling in the throat so that every breath tasted of cold metal.  

The Dry‑Zone Village lay fifteen kilometers south of the Rainline, a cluster of low, blocky modules welded from discarded shipping containers that looked like forgotten building blocks under the stars. Their roofs were sheathed in thin‑film photovoltaics; nocturnal radiative cooling forced atmospheric vapor into fine beads that slid down the condensation towers’ guide channels into storage bladders. Lin Tan moved along the narrow corridor into the village heart, her boots crushing compacted saline‑alkali soil that whispered a soft “sh‑sh” with each step. First came the dry quartz dust in the nose, then the damp, rotten‑moss odor—an abrupt inversion of soil and air that occurred within a single stride across the Rainline. Faded graffiti flickered on the walls in the dim light; a few lines of coordinate numbers had been sand‑blasted into illegibility, only the latest version number still barely legible.  

Aya stood beside a condensation tower, clutching a yellowed slip of paper, her fingertips dusted with moldy paper fibers. She was the village’s leader and the only one who could read the handwritten “Rainline coordinate / Baseline version.” Seeing Lin Tan approach, Aya said nothing, simply handing over the slip. On the back, faded ink read: “V‑3.1 165 mm.” Lin Tan took it, feeling the coarse paper; her pulse synced with the biometric pen’s sensor, and the display still showed **112.3 mm ±0.12**. She whispered, “Reading 112.3 mm, error 0.12, within Rainline standard.” Her knuckles clicked faintly, the habitual mantra following: “Millimeters don’t lie.” A thin smear of pale yellow grease lingered at the slip’s worn edge—the residue from the last time Aya had lubed the tower’s bearings with discarded grease; a touch revealed the slick film.  

Villagers formed a half‑circle, their eyes holding no anger, only a numbness locked inside numbers. Every doorway displayed a travel credential, a blue watermark glowing faintly in the night: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Remaining 312 mm.” Lin Tan withdrew 0.5 mm from her quota card and pressed it into Aya’s palm, fingertips brushing the cold plastic edge; a sour guilt rose—she knew the amount was merely a rounding tail in the ledger, yet enough for a child to swallow one more sip of synthetic protein paste. The Insurance Consortium’s remote audit probes blinked atop the towers, a red beam sweeping the data stream every few seconds, confirming each droplet entered the Carbon‑Meter Dual‑Unit Ledger. The probes’ high‑frequency whine sounded like mosquitoes circling ears; villagers instinctively shrank their necks, but none dared protest.  

Aya murmured, “The archive still has magnetic tapes; the old curator guards the original signed footage.” Before the words faded, a distant maglev pilgrimage bus horn sounded, a low electric hum piercing the mist. The carriage’s advertisement flickered in the darkness: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp of Rising Seas.” The bus moved one‑way into the abandonment zone; its interior reeked of rust and damp mold, while outside abandoned high‑rises jutted like skeletons against the sky. Lin Tan watched the taillights dissolve into the Rainline’s shadow, traced a “∟” gesture on her screen—knuckles clicking again—then changed it to “✓,” shifting the semantics from “confirm” to “witness.” The cursor trembled faintly in the night, as if responding to some inner oath.  

She slipped the tape back into her pack; the zipper’s teeth meshed with a crisp snap in the silence. Her knee stabbed again, a reminder that the road ahead still demanded crossing more invisible lines. Far off, an orbital scavenger shift wrestled with 38 °C heat inside its pod; coolant alarms flashed red, fragment‑capture metrics deciding next year’s Cold Slot priority. Lin Tan regulated her breathing, activated the exoskeleton, and stepped toward Gate 7’s Cold Slot entrance. The saline‑alkali soil crunched under the exoskeleton’s micro‑vibrations, a fine brittle sound like the earth quietly tallying the cost of each step.  

The metal grille at the Cold Slot entrance shuddered in the night wind; the air tasted of ozone and stale grease, a metallic bitterness on the tongue. The exoskeleton’s joints emitted a low hydraulic hiss; the footplates crushed the soil into a fine powder, each step accompanied by a soft “click‑clack” resonating against the eardrums. Lin Tan looked up; a holo‑screen projected a pale‑blue quota ledger into the mist: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Used 888 mm, Remaining 312 mm,” the numbers pulsing under the red scan like a heartbeat. She instinctively pinched her fingertips; knuckles clicked once more, a quiet mantra: “Numbers won’t lie for me.”  

“Pass.” A rasping electronic voice issued from the gate, the sound refracting in the metal conduit into a shrill whine. Lin Tan tapped her quota card to the sensor; the blue watermark lit, the 0.5 mm tail deducted and written into the ledger instantly, the screen flashing “Confirmed ✓.” A chill of plastic brushed her fingertips; the familiar sour guilt surged again—this tail was only a rounding artifact in the ledger, yet it could buy Aya another sip of synthetic protein.  

“Don’t rush, there’s a mag‑rail checkpoint ahead.” A low male voice came from behind, scented with machine oil. Lin Tan turned to see a scavenger in a worn radiation‑shield coat, a still‑smoking coolant pipe in hand, his face etched with wrinkles from years of heat. He offered the pipe: “Take it; keep the coolant alarm from locking you inside.”  

Lin Tan accepted, feeling the faint sear of the metal surface; her brow furrowed then relaxed, knuckles clicking again. She slotted the pipe into the exoskeleton’s heat‑sink; the hydraulic pump’s whine steadied, the red alarm died, green light glowed. The maglev rail beneath her feet emitted a low‑frequency vibration, a giant’s heartbeat propelling her forward through the gate, into the precisely calculated Cold Slot where every meter was logged as a “Carbon‑Meter” cost. The pilgrimage bus’s taillights had long vanished into the Rainline; only a faint blue glow at the rail’s end trembled in the night wind, a promise awaiting the next witness who would count millimeters into bone and blood.  

The mag‑rail checkpoint’s gate glowed a ghostly blue in the fog; the scanner’s red line swept over Lin Tan’s exoskeleton, emitting a short “zzt—” electric snap, then the screen read “Carbon‑Meter 0.12 mm, Quota Synced.” She felt the invisible magnetic field cradle her spine like an unseen hand; each pulse pressed the deep knee ache into a more solid weight. The scavenger returned the coolant pipe to his belt hook, fingertips sparking faintly on the metal clasp, wiping condensed oil‑sweat from his cheek.  

“Will the quota last till next season?” he asked, his voice resonating low in the conduit, tinged with oil and ozone. Lin Tan didn’t raise her head; she merely clicked her knuckles again, the numbers flashing before her eyes: “Remaining 311.5 mm, just enough to get Aya into the next synthetic‑protein tier.” She tapped her quota card to the secondary sensor; blue light flared, another 0.3 mm deducted, the ledger gaining a line “Witness ✓.”  

Ahead the Cold Slot curved into darkness, its surface glazed with a thin frost; the magnetic field ground the saline‑alkali soil into fine sand, each step a soft “sh‑sh” like the earth quietly bookkeeping. In the distance the maglev pilgrimage bus horn sounded again, its advertisement blinking in the night: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp of Rising Seas.” The taillights were swallowed by rain, leaving only that faint blue glimmer at the rail’s terminus, trembling in the night wind, waiting for the next person who would tally millimeters into flesh and blood.
