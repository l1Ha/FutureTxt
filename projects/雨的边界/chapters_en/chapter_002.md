# Chapter 2: The Condensation Water Ledger of the Arid Village

Night wind poured in from the valley mouth, carrying a briny salt‑frost tang mixed with the monotonous tapping of the Condensation Tower’s drips — like someone flicking a row of empty tin cans with a fingernail, each strike blooming into a fine echo in the thin mist. Lin Tan locked her exoskeleton’s micro‑motion pattern into low‑frequency mode; the pain‑relief patch on the inner knee softened under body heat but still leaked a dull ache, reminding her that every crossing of the Rainline exacted a skeletal toll for the data debt. She felt the worn magnetic tape in her pack’s side pouch, its frayed edges exposing copper wire; her fingertips traced the grooves of the data tracks, the sole physical evidence of the unauthorized 0.2 mm upward correction she had made ten years ago. The wind also bore a hint of rusted iron — the scent of abandoned orbital sleepers leaching corrosion in the damp night, shooting straight up the nasal passage to the throat, so that every breath tasted of cold metal.  

The Arid Village lay fifteen kilometers south of the Rainline, a cluster of low, blocky modules welded from discarded shipping containers that glimmered like forgotten Lego bricks under starlight. Their roofs were sheathed in thin‑film photovoltaics; nocturnal radiative cooling coaxed atmospheric vapor into fine beads that slid down the Condensation Tower’s guide channels into storage bladders. Lin Tan slipped through the narrow alley into the village heart, her boots on packed saline‑alkali soil that whispered a soft “shh‑shh” with each step. The air shifted in a single stride: first the dry quartz dust of the desert, then the damp, rotting‑moss odor of the Rainline’s fringe — an abrupt inversion of soil and atmosphere across the invisible boundary. Faded graffiti mottled the walls, coordinates and version numbers scoured by wind‑blown sand until only the latest version tag remained legible.  

Aya stood beside a Condensation Tower, clutching a yellowed slip of paper, her fingertips dusted with mold‑scented fibers. She was the village’s leader, the only one who could read the handwritten “Rainline coordinate / Baseline version”. As Lin Tan approached, Aya said nothing, merely handed over the slip. On the back, faded ink read: “V‑3.1 165 mm”. Lin Tan took it, feeling the coarse paper; her pulse synced with the Biometric Stylus’s sensor, and the display still showed **112.3 mm ±0.12**. She recited quietly: “Reading 112.3 mm, error 0.12, within Rainline standard.” A faint click sounded from her knuckles, her habitual mantra following: “Millimeters don’t lie.” A smear of pale yellow grease lingered at the slip’s worn edge — residue from the last time Aya lubed the tower’s bearings with scavenged grease; a touch revealed the thin, slick film.  

Villagers formed a half‑circle, eyes devoid of anger, only a numbness locked inside numbers. Every doorway displayed a travel credential, a blue watermark glowing in the night: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Remaining 312 mm”. Lin Tan drew 0.5 mm from her Programmable Quota Card and pressed it into Aya’s palm, the cold plastic edge biting her fingertips, a sour guilt rising — she knew the amount was merely a rounding tail in the ledger, yet it could buy a child one more sip of synthetic protein paste. The Insurance Syndicate’s remote audit probes flickered atop the Condensation Tower, a red beam sweeping the data stream every few seconds, confirming each drop entered the Carbon‑Millimeter Dual Unit ledger. The probes’ high‑pitched whine sounded like mosquitoes circling ears; villagers instinctively hunched their shoulders, yet none dared protest.  

Aya murmured, “The Archive still holds magnetic tape; Curator Guan guards the original signing footage.” Before the words faded, a distant Maglev Pilgrimage Bus horn blew, a low electric hum cutting through the mist. The bus’s hull advertisements pulsed in the darkness: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp Swallowed by Rising Seas”. The one‑way bus entered the Abandoned Zone; its interior reeked of rust and damp mold, while outside skeletal skyscrapers pierced the sky like ribs. Lin Tan watched the taillights vanish into the Rainline’s shadow, her fingertip drawing a “∟” gesture on the screen — knuckles clicking again — then shifting to “✓”, the semantics turning from “confirm” to “witness”. The cursor trembled in the night, as if answering a silent oath.  

She slipped the tape back into her pack; the zipper’s teeth meshed with a crisp snap in the silence. Her knee stabbed again, a reminder that the road ahead still demanded crossing more invisible lines. Far off, a Scavenger‑7 crew wrestled 38 °C heat inside their cabin, coolant alarms flashing red; their debris‑capture quota determined next year’s Cold Slot priority. Lin Tan regulated her breathing, engaged the exoskeleton, and stepped toward Gate 7’s Cold Slot entrance. The saline‑alkali soil crunched under the exoskeleton’s micro‑vibrations, a fine, brittle sound like the earth quietly tallying the cost of each stride.  

The metal grille at the Cold Slot entrance shuddered in the night wind, the air laced with ozone and the sour tang of aged lubricant — one breath and you could taste the metallic bitterness of rust. The exoskeleton’s joints emitted a low hydraulic hiss; the footplates crushed the soil into a fine powder, each step accompanied by a soft “click” resonating against the eardrums. Lin Tan lifted her gaze; a holo‑screen projected a pale‑blue quota ledger into the fog: “Annual Quota 1200 mm, Used 888 mm, Remaining 312 mm”, numbers pulsing under the red scan like a heartbeat. She instinctively pinched her fingertips, knuckles clicking once more, whispering: “Numbers won’t lie to me.”  

“Pass.” A rasping electronic voice erupted from the gate, sound waves refracting in the metal conduit into a piercing shriek. Lin Tan tapped her Quota Card to the sensor; the blue watermark flared, the 0.5 mm tail deducted instantly and written to the ledger, the screen flashing “Confirmed ✓”. A chill of plastic brushed her fingertip; the same sour guilt surged — this tail was only a rounding artifact in the ledger, yet it could grant Aya another sip of synthetic protein paste.  

“Don’t rush, there’s a mag‑rail checkpoint ahead.” A low male voice came from behind, breath scented with machine oil. Lin Tan turned to see a scavenger in a worn radiation‑shield coat, a still‑smoking coolant tube clenched in his hand, his face etched with wrinkles from years of heat. He offered the tube: “Take it, keep the coolant alarm from locking you in.”  

Lin Tan accepted, fingertips searing on the metal’s heat, brow furrowing then smoothing, knuckles clicking again. She slotted the tube into the exoskeleton’s heat‑sink; the hydraulic pump’s whine steadied, the red light died, green illuminated. The mag‑lev track thrummed beneath her feet, a low‑frequency pulse like a giant heart, propelling her forward through the gate into the precisely calculated Cold Slot, every meter logged as a “Carbon‑Millimeter Dual Unit” expense. The pilgrimage bus’s taillights had long vanished into the Rainline; only a faint blue glow at the track’s end trembled in the night wind, a promise awaiting the next witness who would count millimeters into bone and blood.  

The mag‑rail checkpoint’s gate glowed a ghostly blue in the mist; the scanner’s red line swept Lin Tan’s exoskeleton, emitting a short “zzzt” of current, then the screen read “Carbon‑Millimeter 0.12 mm, Quota Synced”. She felt the invisible magnetic cradle beneath her soles support her spine; each pulse pressed the deep knee ache deeper. The scavenger returned the coolant tube to his belt clip, fingertips striking tiny sparks on the metal latch, wiping away the oil‑sweat beading on his cheek.  

“Quota enough to last the next season?” he asked, voice reverberating in the conduit as a low resonance, tinged with oil and ozone. Lin Tan didn’t look up; she merely clicked her knuckles again, the numbers flashing before her eyes: “Remaining 311.5 mm, just enough to get Aya into the next synthetic‑protein tier.” She tapped her Quota Card to the secondary sensor; blue light flared, 0.3 mm deducted, the ledger gaining a line “Witness ✓”.  

Ahead, the Cold Slot curved into shadow, its surface glazed with a thin frost; the magnetic field compressed the saline soil into fine sand, each step a soft “shh‑shh” like the earth quietly bookkeeping. In the distance the Maglev Pilgrimage Bus horn sounded again, its hull ads flickering in the night: “Revisit 2035, Witness the Last Streetlamp Swallowed by Rising Seas”. Taillights were swallowed by rain, leaving only that faint blue glow at the track’s terminus, trembling in the night wind, waiting for the next soul who would etch millimeters into marrow.
