# Chapter 4: The Orbital Scavenger's Cold Slot Window

The coolant pipes lining the bulkhead throbbed with a low, muffled pulse in micro‑gravity, like a suppressed heart striking the metal cavity beat by beat. Red warning lamps flashed twice per second, bleaching the capture sensors on Lao Chen’s right‑arm prosthesis a ghastly white. The air reeked of ozone mixed with overheated lubricant, a metallic scorched smell; each breath felt like shoving a soldering iron into the nostrils, searing the nasal mucosa and slipping down the trachea into the lungs. The analgesic patch on Lin Tan’s knee hissed faintly beneath sweat‑slick skin, the old injury on the inner side of her right knee throbbing in time with the hull’s micro‑vibration, reminding her that a full decade had passed since she last crossed the Rainline.  

She pressed her fingertips against the condensation layer on the bulkhead, feeling a thin veil of cold — a silent reminder that the temperature here forever hovered on the edge of the critical point. The air‑circulation pump droned at a low frequency, laced with the faint metallic grit left by the last particulate‑filter change, a scent identical to the lingering embers of a ground‑side shop that had just cut titanium alloy. Lin Tan closed her eyes, trying to separate that odor from the mossy, damp stench of the Rainline’s southern flank ten years ago, only to find them woven together in the deep texture of memory — wet cold soil, fermenting leaf litter, and the faint sweetness of distant water vapor condensing into dew.  

“The Cold Slot waits for no one.” Lao Chen’s voice squeezed out of the comms, laced with the whine of his prosthetic arm’s motors, delivered at the rapid clip of someone reciting code long since memorized, yet its tail carried an almost imperceptible weariness: “Capture, lock, withdraw. Thermal budget 2 MW — data upload consumes 1.3, debris task still needs 0.9. You write the data to the Ledger first; I’ll free 0.4 for the laser capture.”  

Lin Tan didn’t reply. She pulled the Portable Laser Disdrometer from her pocket; the probe vibrated against her fingertip, the display frozen at **112.3 mm ±0.12** — the last reading she had taken at Hydrological Station 112, its error band still within tolerance. She pressed the Biometric Stylus to the reader; the screen flashed **“Identity verified ✓”**, then she traced the fixed **「∟」** Signing Gesture across the surface, her knuckles clicking softly. The data stream slipped into the fiber like liquid mercury, and the double‑blind signature module lit up in red: **“Disputed version·pending adjudication”.**  

The cabin thermometer climbed to **38 °C**, the coolant’s red pulse growing more frantic. The sensors on Lao Chen’s right‑arm prosthesis emitted a piercing whine; the capture arm traced a blue‑white streak through vacuum, and as the seventh 3‑cm‑class fragment seated in the gripper, the arm’s joints groaned with metal fatigue, a low roar like a string about to snap. He gritted his teeth and growled, “Give me thirty more seconds — open the radiator valves full.”  

Lao Chen’s breathing grew ragged over the comms; each inhalation was accompanied by the hiss of the prosthetic’s internal hydraulic pump, fluid hammering against metal walls with a dull *thud‑thud*. Through the viewport Lin Tan saw the starfield sliced into countless fine segments by the debris trails, a torn net whose edges refracted starlight into cold, blue‑white blades. She recalled the contrast on the ground across the Rainline: the northern side dry, grit slipping through fingertips, the scent of quartz dust and arid wind — breathing it felt like swallowing a handful of fine sand; the southern side slick and mossy, the odor climbing the nasal passage to the throat, red loam clinging to boot soles, each step a viscous drag. She had once slapped a transdermal analgesic patch on the Rainline; now, in orbit, the residues of both smells lingered, as if the crossing had traversed not merely latitude but two entirely different logics of survival.  

A terse ground‑control directive came over the comms, flat as an automated broadcast: “Thermostat Calibration zone credentials verified; Cold Slot window extended to 06:00.” Lin Tan swiped the Programmable Quota Card, reading **“Annual quota 1200 mm, remaining 312 mm”**, and noted a single sip’s allowance — **0.02 quota** — in the memo field, a reminder that children in the arid villages survived on the **4 L** dripped daily from the Condensation Towers. As her fingertip brushed the screen, micro‑gravity made the characters waver like dry leaves stirred by an unseen wind.  

Lao Chen’s manipulator finally locked the last fragment; cabin temperature dropped two degrees, the red glow shifting to a steady amber. He exhaled, his tone unusually gentle: “Data written to the Ledger, disputed version flagged, countdown 47:59:12.” A network‑wide crimson alert flashed at the center of the display — **“Baseline tampering detected; dial‑turn authority frozen, countdown 47:59:12”.**  

The alert’s red afterimage burned on Lin Tan’s retina; she felt the old knee injury stirred again by the frozen chill, yet she no longer trembled. She withdrew her fingertip, brushing it along the edge of the hatch, the micro‑vibration syncing with her heartbeat. She whispered the professional mantra: “Millimeters do not lie.”  

Lao Chen inserted the capture arm’s tip probe into the bulkhead maintenance port; blue‑white arcs erupted in vacuum, scattering fine sparks that illuminated the dense lattice of scratches on his prosthesis — each groove a record of a capture, of a brush with death.  

“Authority freeze isn’t a lockout,” Lao Chen’s voice carried a metallic rasp. “Curator Guan destroyed the logs because that mold‑spotted page fragment concealed the Rainline’s true Baseline. Export the disputed version, mount it on backup node 03; I’ll dismantle the dial’s physical lock.”  

Lin Tan nodded, her fingertip sliding across the Quota Card screen over “Export‑Dispute‑Node 03”, the characters wavering in micro‑gravity like dry leaves in an unseen wind. She spoke into the comms: “`9‑A‑4‑F‑7‑C‑2‑E`, checksum passed, countdown 47:55:03.” The ground controller’s metallic echo returned: “Received. Physical key authorized; Rainline control transferred to backup node, standing by to open valve.”  

Lao Chen withdrew the titanium‑hex rod; the dial emitted a dull *clack*. Cabin temperature spiked three degrees, the coolant pump’s whine settling into a steady pulse, like a heart regaining its rhythm. He tossed the key into his magnetic catch box; the lid snapped shut with a soft magnetic click, like a heart completing a contraction in a metal chamber. Lin Tan gripped the Quota Card, the cold at her fingertips hardening into a rigid cadence, synced to the red countdown numbers burning on her retina — she knew that for the next forty‑eight hours the Rainline would be rewritten by their own hands.  

The maintenance port on the bulkhead still vented pale blue arcs; the coolant pump’s low‑frequency pulse faltered for a single heartbeat, then shuddered at a frequency never felt before — as if the entire orbit were holding its breath, awaiting the next droplet’s landing. Lao Chen turned, the faint blue light reflected in his eyes, and murmured, “The Rainline is breathing; we decide where the next drop falls.” Lin Tan did not answer; she simply pressed the Quota Card against her chest, feeling the metal’s cold seep into her palm, the countdown ticking on, red numbers burning on her retina.
