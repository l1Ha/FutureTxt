# Chapter 4: The Orbital Scavengers' Cold Slot Window

The coolant pipes lining the bulkhead thrummed with a low, muffled pulse in micro‑gravity, like a restrained heart striking the metal cavity beat by beat. Red alarm lamps flashed twice per second, bleaching the capture sensors on Old Chen’s right‑arm prosthesis a sickly white. The air reeked of ozone mixed with overheated lubricant, a metallic scorch that turned every breath into a soldering iron shoved up the nostrils, searing the mucosa and slipping down the trachea into the lungs. The analgesic patch stuck to Lin Tan’s knee hissed faintly beneath sweat‑slick skin; the old injury on the inner side of her right knee throbbed dully with each微振 of the hull, reminding her that a full decade had passed since she last crossed the Rainline.  

She pressed her fingertips against the condensation layer on the bulkhead, feeling a thin veneer of cold—a silent reminder that the temperature here forever hovered on the knife‑edge of criticality. The air‑circulation pump droned at a low frequency, laced with the faint metallic grit left behind when the particulate filters were swapped, a scent identical to the embers left after cutting titanium alloy in a ground‑side maintenance bay. Lin Tan closed her eyes, trying to separate that odor from the mossy, brine‑laden air of the Rainline’s southern fringe ten years ago, only to find the two intertwining deep in her nasal passages into a single texture of memory: damp cold soil, fermenting leaf litter, the faint sweetness of dew condensing on distant vapor.  

“Cold Slot doesn’t wait.” Old Chen’s voice squeezed through the comms, laced with the electric whine of his prosthetic’s motors, delivered at the speed of a well‑rehearsed script, yet its tail carried a barely perceptible weariness: “Capture, lock, withdraw. Thermal budget two megawatts, data upload consumes 1.3, debris task needs 0.9. You write the data to the ledger first; I’ll free 0.4 for the laser capture.”  

Lin Tan didn’t reply. She pulled the Portable Laser Raindrop Spectrometer (W‑12) from a pocket; the probe trembled minutely at her fingertip, the display frozen at **112.3 mm ±0.12**—the last reading she had taken at Hydrological Station 112, still within tolerance. She pressed the biometric stylus to the reader; the screen flashed **“Identity confirmed ✓”**, then she traced the fixed **「∟」** gesture across the glass, joints clicking softly. The data stream slid into the fiber like liquid mercury; the dual‑blind signature module lit up in red: **“Disputed version·Pending arbitration.”**  

The cabin temperature gauge climbed to **38 ℃**; the coolant’s red pulse grew more frantic. Old Chen’s prosthetic sensors shrieked, the capture arm carving a blue‑white streak through vacuum. When the seventh 3‑cm fragment clamped into the jaws, the arm’s joints emitted a dull metallic groan, like a piano string about to snap. He gritted his teeth and growled: “Give me thirty more seconds—thermal valves wide open.”  

Old Chen’s breathing grew ragged in the comms, each inhale accompanied by the hydraulic pump’s hiss inside the prosthesis, fluid hammering against metal walls with a heavy “thud, thud.” Through the viewport Lin Tan saw the starfield sliced into countless fine segments by the debris trails, a torn net where starlight refracted cold, blue‑white edges along fragment rims. She recalled the contrast on the ground: the northern side of the Rainline—dry soil, quartz dust slipping through fingertips, the scent of silica and arid wind, each breath like swallowing a handful of fine sand; the southern side—clammy, moss‑laden, the aroma climbing the nasal passage to the throat, red laterite clinging to soles, every step a viscous resistance. Back then she had slapped a transdermal analgesic patch on the Rainline itself; now, in orbit, the residue of those two smells lingered, as if she had crossed not merely latitude but two entirely different survival logics.  

A terse ground‑control directive crackled over the comms, flat as an automated broadcast: “Thermal‑zone transit credentials verified; Cold Slot window extended to 06:00.” Lin Tan glanced at her Programmable Quota Card: **“Annual quota 1200 mm, remaining 312 mm.”** She logged a single sip’s allowance—**0.02 quota**—in the memo, a reminder that children in the arid villages survived on the **4 L** dripped daily from the Condensation Towers. In micro‑gravity the characters on the screen shivered, like dry leaves stirred by an invisible wind.  

Old Chen’s arm finally locked the last fragment; cabin temperature dropped two degrees, the red glow steadied to a stable amber. He exhaled long, voice unusually gentle: “Data written to ledger, marked disputed version, countdown 47:59:12.” A fleet‑wide crimson alert blossomed center‑screen—**“Baseline tampering detected; dial‑turn authority frozen; countdown 47:59:12.”**  

The alarm’s afterimage burned on Lin Tan’s retina; the old knee injury flared again under the sudden chill, yet she no longer trembled. She withdrew her fingertips, brushing the edge of the hatch; the micro‑vibration synced with her heartbeat. In a low murmur she repeated the professional mantra: “Millimeters don’t lie.”  

Old Chen inserted the probe of his capture arm into the bulkhead maintenance port; a blue‑white arc erupted in vacuum, scattering fine sparks that illuminated the dense lattice of scratches on his prosthetic—each groove a record of a capture, each near‑miss a brush with death.  

“Authority frozen doesn’t mean locked,” Old Chen’s voice carried a metallic rasp. “The Old Curator burned the logs because that mold‑stained scrap of paper hid the true Rainline baseline. Export the disputed version, hang it on backup node 03; I’ll strip the dial’s physical lock.”  

Lin Tan nodded, sliding her fingertip across the quota‑card screen to **“Export‑Dispute‑Node 03”**; the characters wavered in micro‑gravity like dry leaves in an unseen breeze. She spoke into the comms: “9‑A‑4‑F‑7‑C‑2‑E, checksum verified, countdown 47:55:03.” The ground controller’s metallic echo returned: “Received; physical key authorized; Rainline control transferred to backup node; ready to open valve.”  

Old Chen withdrew a titanium‑coated hex rod; the dial gave a heavy “clunk.” Cabin temperature spiked three degrees; the coolant pump’s whine settled into a steady pulse, like a heart rediscovering its rhythm. He tossed the key into his magnetic snap‑box; the lid sealed with a soft magnetic click, a heartbeat in a metal chamber. Lin Tan clenched the quota card, the cold at her fingertips hardening into a firm cadence, syncing with the scarlet countdown burning on her retina. She knew the next forty‑eight hours would see the Rainline rewritten by their own hands.  

The maintenance port still sputtered faint blue arcs; the coolant pump’s low‑frequency pulse faltered for a single cardiac interval, then shuddered at a frequency never felt before—as if the entire orbit held its breath, awaiting the next droplet’s landing. Old Chen turned, eyes reflecting that weak blue glow, voice low: “The Rainline is breathing; we decide where the next drop falls.” Lin Tan offered no answer, only pressed the quota card against her chest, feeling the metal’s chill seep into her palm, the countdown ticking on, red numbers blazing across her vision.
