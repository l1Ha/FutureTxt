# Chapter 6: The Arbitration Court's 48‑Hour Game

Cold air seeped from the holographic walls, carrying the scent of ozone and metal coolant into the nostrils, settling on Lin Tan’s skin like a thin frost; a low‑frequency hum rose from the floor, vibrating the bones of her feet, and a faint static prickle tingled at her fingertips when they brushed the edge of the signing screen. A stale rust odor still drifted in the air—the musty, damp breath that had accumulated year after year in the carriages of the Pilgrimage Buses from the Abandoned Zone, seeping through the ventilation ducts into the conference room, reminding every person seated at the dials that the land beyond the rain line had long since cracked into a salt crust.  

The conference room was oval. In its center hovered a real‑time rain‑line projection, blue‑green contour lines trembling slightly with each data pulse. Engineers from the Three Major Blocs, monitors from the Global Thermostat Treaty Organization, and actuaries from the Insurance Consortium formed a circle, each with a biometric fingerprint ring glowing coldly on their knuckles. Presiding Judge Hermann sat at the head, the ring on his left ring finger pulsing in time with his heartbeat, his right index finger tapping the table in a crisp “click‑click,” as if timing the imminent double‑blind signatures. Data chips scattered across the tabletop condensed tiny droplets in the chill, refracting the hidden fiber‑optic array above.  

Lin Tan’s old knee injury tightened with the draft from the air‑conditioning; the analgesic patch hissed against sweat‑slick skin, a curled corner of the adhesive turning cool and tacky under her fingertip. She withdrew the W‑12 Portable Laser Raindrop Spectrometer from her pocket; its probe lit up, the screen still showing the same figure: 112.3 mm ± 0.12. For twenty years she had verified that number on this same instrument over a thousand times; today she performed each verification step with deliberate slowness—fingerprint, gesture, the clicking knuckles.  

“Still 112.3,” she said. Her voice fell into the holographic wall’s echo, stretched, flattened, until only the digits remained.  

Presiding Judge Hermann’s gaze swept the three delegations, his tone even and measured: “Authorization chain **W1** has completed double‑blind signing; the log has been written to the Carbon Ledger. The 27th dial‑turn window has 48 hours remaining; any party that vetoes twice consecutively within 48 h will trigger **W7** climate mutually assured destruction.” His voice carried a mechanical flatness; a fingertip brushed his fingerprint ring, the light band shifting from cyan to violet.  

North American Federation engineer Mark spoke first, fingers dancing across the holographic keyboard: “We submit a veto on the grounds that baseline version V‑3.1 shows signs of tampering; the dial must be paused pending archive verification.” A red line appeared on the screen: **Veto 1/2**.  

Eurasian Alliance monitor Ye Lin frowned, her fingertip lingering on her ring before confirming: “Agree to pause; require original magnetic tapes on‑chain within 24 hours.” A second red line lit: **Veto 2/2**.  

East Asian Consortium actuary Sun Hao fell silent for three seconds, fingertip hovering over the confirm key, micro‑data streams flickering across his brow. He murmured, “If we delay further, the Cold Slot auction will miss this quarter’s window; the compute‑exchange rate will fluctuate more than 7 %.” He finally pressed, and **“Pending Confirmation”** replaced the third red line.  

A dull ache rose deep in Lin Tan’s right knee; the patch’s adhesive edge curled further in the sweat. She inhaled sharply, palm sweat evaporating on the metal surface, leaving a thin crust of salt. The holographic rain line before her drifted imperceptibly; the ±0.5 mm contour at the blue‑green boundary rose and fell like breath.  

Ten years earlier flashed in her mind: at Station 112 she had altered the reading from 112.5 mm to 112.3 mm, hoping to grant a friend’s district an extra 0.2 mm quota; the result was that the district was judged “over‑quota” that season, its entire farmland frozen for three years, and the friend lost the whole harvest to drought. What she heard then was not protest but the whisper of wind over cracked earth, like nails on a blackboard. Now the same fingertips hovered over the same screen; the only differences were the knee’s old scar and the countdown ticking on the display.  

“Numbers don’t lie,” she whispered internally, then aloud to the holographic wall: “The data won’t deceive me.”  

The countdown in the corner ticked down in seconds: **47:59:12**. The Carbon Ledger’s blockchain‑style log refreshed in sync; each signature was accompanied by a low, resonant electronic chirp, like a heartbeat transmitted through the floor. Old Chen’s encrypted message flashed: Cold Slot postponed to 06:30, log on‑chained. Lin Tan’s fingertip twitched; the “∟” gesture blinked beneath the cursor, ready to become a “✓”.  

Presiding Judge Hermann’s fingerprint ring abruptly flared deep crimson. He rose before the holographic projection, his chair emitting a faint groan of metal fatigue: “If consensus cannot be reached within the remaining 48 hours, the Sunshade Ring will open fully, the Alkalinity Pumps will shut down completely, and global precipitation will shift instantly by more than 15 %.” His voice reverberated through the room, carrying an irreversible finality.  

Lin Tan felt the static prickle at her fingertips intensify; the countdown jumped to **47:58:03**. She pressed the W‑12 probe to her forehead; the faint laser traced a thin line across her retina, as if inscribing a ruler for the impending verdict. On either side of the holographic rain line, the scents of dry quartz dust and damp moss rose simultaneously in memory, compressed into this cramped conference room at the same instant.  

The communicator sounded again; the dispatcher’s cold female voice pierced the encryption layer: “Countdown 00:02:17, please confirm final signing authority.” Lin Tan drew a deep breath, tapped the card surface three times, and the screen popped up “Sign Confirmation·Effective.” The amber light band snapped to deep blue; a low chime resonated like a bell struck inside her chest.  

The holographic rain line froze instantly; the blue‑green boundary ceased its drift. Lin Tan’s fingertip remained suspended over the signing screen, gesture uncompleted; the room was left only with the low electronic chirps and the continuous whine of the air‑conditioning, as if the planet’s entire water cycle had held its breath. A sudden sharp spike of pain shot through Lin Tan’s right knee; the analgesic patch finally peeled away completely, revealing a small patch of inflamed skin. She did not reach to press it, merely let sweat slide down her jaw and drop onto the cold metal tabletop with a clear “drip—tap.”  

The wall projection flickered; the countdown digits trembled faintly. Lin Tan heard, from far away, a low hydraulic valve opening.
