# Chapter 8: The Boundary of Rain

The morning wind slipped through the window seams of the *Polaris* module, first a dry, cold metallic tang, then a damp, mossy scent—as if two air masses of different temperature were wrestling in the narrow corridor. Lin Tan leaned against the headboard; her fingertips still felt the faint residual vibration of the W‑12 probe, a subtle tremor climbing the phalanges to her wrist, carrying the characteristic chill of an instrument. The old injury on the inner side of her right knee no longer stabbed sharply when she crossed the rainline; the analgesic patch had long been peeled away, leaving only a dull warmth deep in the joint, as though her body had finally learned to coexist with that breathing frontier. She had never activated a longevity subscription; the “Subscription Status: Inactive” line on her medical report still glared, the reason unchanged—the words etched in her heart: *I don’t want to hand the pain in my knee over to an algorithm.*  

The low‑temperature radiator panels on the bulkhead glowed a faint blue, stretching her shadow into a thin silver line. Fine iron filings floated in the air; each breath tasted of rust in the throat, mingled with a faint chlorine seep from the recirculating water pipes. Pressure waves from the circulation pump struck the metal ribs at 0.7 Hz, a heartbeat‑like micro‑tremor she could feel by pressing a fingertip to the pipe wall. She ran her hand along the alloy frame of the bunk, the cold hardness confirming that this spot remained her own, an immutable physical coordinate that could not be remotely overwritten.  

Outside the holographic projection, the floating band drifted northward at 0.03° per hour; the blue‑green interface pulsed like a living breath, its width oscillating between ±0.8 mm and ±2.3 mm. Pixels at the projection’s edge flickered in the dawn light, as if a needle‑fine tip were repeatedly piercing the atmospheric membrane. Lin Tan rose; her exoskeleton emitted a soft hydraulic hiss at the ankles, the gear meshing rhythm resonating inexplicably with her own pulse. She walked step by step toward the small memorial platform at the module’s far end, each footfall leaving a shallow dent in the metal deck—silent coordinates for those who would follow.  

Atop the platform lay a gray‑white stele, inscribed with a friend’s name and the date of the arbitration ten years past. Wind‑erosion had honeycombed the stone’s edges into a fine lattice of pores; fingertips tracing them felt a faint roughness, as though every groove whispered the vows washed by rain yet never forgotten. From her pocket she withdrew a worn metal clasp, its edges smoothed by rain and sweat into a gentle curve; rubbing it produced a faint “shhh” like an ancient oath murmuring at her fingertips. A trace of pale blue fluorescent powder still clung to the clasp’s inner rim—the marker the Arbitration Tribunal had scattered to denote “Rainline Passage Rights”—still glowing weakly in the dim light.  

“112.3 mm.” She whispered the figure, the sound reverberating in the cramped cabin with a barely perceptible tremor. Raindrops began to strike the stele; the first landed in an engraved groove with a clear “ding,” followed by a second, a third, forming thin rivulets that traced the stone’s veins, carrying away dust and the decade’s accumulated guilt. Each impact set off an almost inaudible resonance within the stone, as if the earth itself were answering her testimony. Lin Tan pressed her palm to the stele’s edge, fingertips sensing the cool moisture seeping from the honeycomb pores; the screen automatically logged “Confirmed and Witnessed,” the carbon ledger silently writing a new timestamp in the background. At the same instant, the floating‑band algorithm recorded the scents of the soils on both sides—dry‑land mineral dust and damp moss—into the database, data streams sliding through fiber at light speed, yet leaving a tangible warmth at her fingertips.  

Far off, the Orbital Sunshade Ring trembled faintly in the dawn, its metal struts emitting a fine fatigue creak, like a colossal organism stretching its limbs in preparation for the next breath of the rainline. The ring’s coating shimmered with oil‑slick iridescence; each micro‑vibration sent light spots dancing across the bulkhead, projecting fractured geometric patterns. Lin Tan inhaled deeply; cold air slid down her trachea, mixing dust, moss, iron rust, and damp mold, while her fingertips still registered the probe’s lingering micro‑vibration—as if the planet’s entire hydrological cycle were concentrated in this moment of touch.  

A faint magnetic‑boot scuff sounded behind her, then a low male voice cut through the chill dampness: “Lin Tan, the sensor array on the rainline just reported an anomaly—humidity gradient jumped 12 % in three seconds.”  

She did not turn, merely kept her hand on the stele’s edge, feeling the cool vapor seep from the honeycomb pores, as if confirming an ancient pact. “It’s the old ‘Rainline Passage Rights’ marker leaking,” she said calmly, though a hint of hoarseness lingered. “The fluorescent powder the Tribunal scattered ten years ago seeped into the strata with the rain; geothermal heat is now forcing it back to the surface.”  

The man approached, magnetic boots leaving a faint magnetic trace on the deck, his exoskeleton’s knee joints clicking softly. He was Chen Mo, once Lin Tan’s partner at the Floating‑Band Administration, now head of the Boundary Monitoring Group. His pupils reflected the hologram’s data streams; the old scar on his brow caught a faint rust hue in the morning light. “The data’s already uploaded to the carbon ledger; the algorithm classifies this as ‘natural seepage,’ not triggering the ‘human intervention’ clause.” He paused, swiping a “✗” on his wrist console, his tone weary yet restrained. “But I’ve requested a manual review, citing the still‑active ‘Historical Oath’ clause. W‑12B array sampling at 200 Hz, power draw 0.8 W, error band ±0.3 %.”  

Lin Tan finally turned, her gaze sweeping over the badge on Chen Mo’s chest—a silver raindrop etched into a honeycomb pattern by rain, its rim still dusted with the blue fluorescent powder. She caught his scent: lubricating oil, ozone, and the distinctive damp moss that only years of patrolling the floating band could impart. “Do you still remember the oath?” she asked, fingertips tapping the stele’s surface, each tap stirring an almost inaudible resonance within the stone.  

Chen Mo fell silent for a few seconds; his breath fogged inside his mask before the air purifier whisked it away. “I remember,” he replied quietly, his cadence like a final firmware upgrade to an aging server. “‘As long as the rainline keeps breathing, I’ll be here, guarding this boundary for you.’ But now the boundary isn’t just latitude and longitude—it’s every drop of rain, every gram of carbon, every algorithmic verdict.”  

The distant Sunshade Ring shuddered again; its coating refracted shattered rainbows, light spots dancing on the bulkhead, stretching and twisting the two shadows until they merged into a single blurred line. Lin Tan retrieved the worn clasp from her pocket; the blue glow inside its rim pulsed faintly in the morning mist, like a heart refusing to stop. She fastened the clasp into one of the stele’s honeycomb pores; metal met stone with a soft “clink,” instantly swallowed by the rain’s roar.  

“Review cleared.” Chen Mo confirmed on his wrist console; the screen flashed “Historical Oath Clause Active, Boundary Locked.” The carbon ledger settled in sync; the floating‑band algorithm updated the soil‑scent data on both sides—dry‑land mineral dust and damp moss—and this time the data stream carried an extra handwritten line: *Witnesses: Lin Tan, Chen Mo; the rainline still breathes.*  

Far away, the Orbital Sunshade Ring trembled once more in the dawn, as if readying the next breath of the rainline.
