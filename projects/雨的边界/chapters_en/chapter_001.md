# Chapter 1: Millimetres on Both Sides of the Rainline

Mist pressed the plateau into a damp fingerprint, wrapping Hydrological Station 112 on the eastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau. Wind funneled through the pass, carrying the icy scent of pine needles; it hissed softly as it slipped through the nasal passages. Gravel crunched under boot soles with a fine “shh‑shh,” and the fingertip’s contact with the probe sent a micro‑vibration through the palm, like a slender nerve pulsing in the hand. Lin Tan inhaled air tinged with rust, the old ache on the inner side of her right knee flaring faintly. She deftly tore a transdermal analgesic patch from her pocket, pressed it onto the knee, and brushed her fingertips over the worn metal clasp on her cuff—an imprint left by the arbitration ten years ago. The adhesive stiffened in the cold, delivering a brief, cool sting before body heat softened it, as if a rusted key were being slipped back into a familiar lock.

She withdrew the W‑12 Portable Laser Raindrop Spectrometer from her pack; the screen glowed a pale blue, counting down three seconds for calibration. The probe locked onto the baseline points on either side of the rainline; the laser beam cut an invisible line through the mist, as though carving an ultra‑fine incision in the air. The reading settled at **112.3 mm ± 0.12**. Lin Tan murmured, “Reading 112.3 mm, error 0.12, conforms to W2.” The biometric pen in the lower‑right corner of the display flashed; heart‑rate sync confirmed, fingerprint and heartbeat dual‑auth passed, a green check flickered on the retinal projection. The instrument’s internal fan emitted a low hum, like a slumbering beast breathing; each rotation dispersed a faint warmth into the surrounding chill. The vibration traveled through the metal housing to her palm, a subtle electric tingle at the fingertips, echoing a heartbeat inside the metal.

The rainline lay right here: to the north the soil was loose, gravel mixed with fine white quartz powder that felt like crushed glass between the fingers; a thin gray‑white dust slipped through the gaps and drifted on the wind. Only a step to the south, the red earth was wet and sticky, the scent of moss rising sharply; fingertips sank into the mud, pulling out a cool thread of slurry that stretched like ancient glue. Lin Tan collected three samples on each side, writing the data to local cache and noting “traceability required” in the remarks. The virtual keyboard responded with crisp, decisive taps; each keystroke pulsed a faint blue light on the screen, as if timestamping every millimetre recorded. The sample caps clicked shut with a soft “click,” the metal threads biting tightly in the cold, a faint tingling numbness spreading through the fingertips.

The instrument’s micro‑vibration traveled up her arm to her shoulder; Lin Tan’s right knee throbbed more sharply with each crossing of the rainline. She habitually drew a “∟” gesture on the display before closing a case, the knuckles clicking softly, and the mantra followed: “Millimetres don’t lie.” Data does not speak; she only reads numbers, reports errors, cites clauses. The knee pain is a fine needle, each flexion and extension reminding her that measurement is not abstract digits but the compromise of bone and tendon under physical law. Between breaths, mist coiled overhead, cold air seeping through the collar, raising a fine stippling of gooseflesh on her skin.

She returned the spectrometer to her pack, fingertips brushing the metal clasp on the cuff; the cold touch recalled the arbitration a decade ago: a friend’s district had its quota frozen for three years because she had privately corrected the reading upward by 0.2 mm, a fact still unknown to anyone. The knee’s ache reminded her that every crossing of the rainline exacts a price for that decision. The nylon strap of the pack cut a shallow groove across her shoulder, a silent contract reminding her that each measurement repays the data’s debt with flesh. Wind tore the distant pine roar into fragments, mingled with a low metallic groan, as if the entire range were murmuring over that tiny deviation.

An encrypted message popped up on the screen, sender tagged “Old Chen.” The brief text bore the characteristic directive tone of the Orbital Scavengers: “Cold Slot window 02:00‑06:00 available, bring the tape.” Lin Tan’s pupils contracted; she drew a “∟” on the display, then froze the gesture mid‑air, knuckles clicking again—this time she did not confirm immediately, letting the cursor hover between “✓” and “∟.” The mist continued to swallow near and far outlines; only the instrument’s fan drone and the deep dull ache in her knee reminded her that the next step would still be decided by millimetres.

Wind drove the taste of sea salt and ozone into her nostrils; the fan’s low hum trembled against her eardrums. Lin Tan tightened the pack straps, the nylon biting a deeper white line across her collarbone; her fingertip met the cool glass of the screen, like touching a thin sheet of ice. She did not press confirm; instead she raised her wrist, letting the blue watermark of the Programmable Quota Card flicker in the fog—annual quota 1200 mm, remaining 312 mm, the numbers projecting faint blue specks on her retina that brightened and dimmed with each breath. The specks refracted tiny rainbows in her pupils, as if every millimetre were quietly calculating the balance of survival.

“Old Chen, received,” she whispered into the comm, voice shredded by the wind. “Cold Slot window 02:00‑06:00, tape in the side pocket. Send coordinates.” A harsh, metallic laugh crackled back, laced with the Scavengers’ signature electronic screech: “Don’t let the knee slow you down, Lin Tan. Numbers won’t lie for me, and the track beneath my feet won’t wait.” The link cut; an encrypted coordinate string appeared, a red dot tracing a slender arc across the fog‑veil toward the seventh maintenance shaft of the abandoned maglev hub. The red point left an afterimage on her retina, like an un‑drawn thorn reminding her that every step ahead had been precisely measured.

The dull knee ache sharpened with each deep breath, as though a fine nail were driven beneath the patella; every flexion and extension reiterated that measurement is not abstract numbers but bone and tendon compromising under physical law. She shoved the spectrometer deep into the pack, fingertips contacting the cold metal shell of the tape; the chill climbed the fingers, up the arm, to the scapula. Wind scrambled the light spots of the holographic billboard, blue glare stitching the fragmented characters “Revisit 2035” across the grey‑white sky, mocking everyone still bargaining over millimetres. The projection flickered in the mist, its halos refracting into broken geometry within her pupils.

Lin Tan regulated her breathing; the knee emitted a muted clunk, like old gears meshing. She took the first step, boot sole grinding on the north side’s loose quartz powder, the grit whispering “shh‑shh” beneath the tread, then crossed the invisible rainline; the south side’s wet red earth instantly clung to her ankle, mud pulling fine threads that wrapped around her trouser cuff. Each step repays the data’s debt with flesh; each flexion pays for the unauthorized 0.2 mm correction ten years ago. The musk of mud mingled with the dry scent of quartz, forming a composite odor unique to this rainline.

The distant maglev’s low roar rolled over again, electric hiss blended with sea‑salt tang drilling into her ears. Lin Tan switched off the quota‑card projection; the blue watermark vanished into the mist, leaving only the faint glow of her fingertip suspended between “✓” and “∟,” a star that could extinguish at any moment. She glanced down at her knee; the deep dull ache persisted, reminding her: the next step, too, will be decided by millimetres. Mist coiled around her feet, as if trying to swallow every millimetre of distance, yet her footprints alternated clearly on the two sides, sharply etched into the damp soil.
