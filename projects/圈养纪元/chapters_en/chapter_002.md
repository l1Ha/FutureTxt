# Chapter 2: The Grinding of Fragment Exchange

At the far end of the corridor, the automated cleaning robot 07‑T’s treads rolled across the alloy floor, emitting a steady low‑frequency grinding sound laced with a faint sweet mist of lubricant oil.  

Old Chen pushed the maintenance cart; its wheels on the same track produced a duller “clunk” than the robot, metal bearings whispering a fine fatigue note in the cold alloy seams. The data port behind his left ear flickered weakly, and he tapped the handle twice with his fingertips — the pre‑arranged “window‑open” signal for Subject 7‑0423. The tap’s force was calibrated to register a minute capacitance shift without tripping an alarm. The upgrade window (W12) opened only 0.7 seconds every 30 days, the sole interval permitting unauthorized writes; Old Chen had seeded this gap in the cleaning robot’s firmware, masking the extra command behind the rule “execute preset checklist only,” so the firmware log would show only a routine “environment scan complete.” He glanced down at the blinking green light on the hidden panel beneath the cart, his fingertips brushing the cold edge and feeling a faint current crawl up the back of his hand, then he pushed the cart forward, its wheels leaving a string of shallow impressions in the track.  

Subject 7‑0423 moved along the wall, the ankle cuff tightening minutely with each step, the metal clasp leaving a faint imprint on his skin — each contraction a reminder that his body no longer belonged to him. Three parallel scars on his left palm glowed a dull crimson in the dim light; beads of blood seeped from his nail beds, trembling in the pre‑spasm of his nerves, and a cool trace followed each droplet as it slid between his fingers. The retinal ledger’s blood‑red numbers scrolled across the center of his pupil: principal 1.2 × 10⁵ Credit Points (CR) compounding at millisecond intervals, the interest increment visibly swelling each second, the crimson fluid seeming ready to rupture the retinal membrane. He dared not look up at Regulator 9‑A “Vera”’s projection ring; the micro‑ring implanted in her right iris glowed blue in the gloom, auditing data integrity in real time (W8). Any read/write latency exceeding 2 milliseconds would trigger a “Data Integrity Check” and lock the subject for 24 hours. Wall sconces cast long, thin shadows like countless invisible hands measuring his every step; the shadows’ edges trembled on the metal floor, as if poised to close in at any moment.  

Little Six (Subject 7‑0419) leaned against the corner railing, his neck pulse ring flashing in time with his heartbeat, a weak blue light refracting off the metal into shattered prisms that danced across the walls like a fragmented cipher. His language centers had atrophied; he could only tap rhythms with his ankle cuff: two short, one long, pause, then three short — the syntax of “Fragment Exchange” agreed upon for the monitoring blind spot, each beat indexing an encrypted memory fragment. The taps echoed down the corridor, mingling with the robot’s low growl. Old Chen pressed the confirm key on the hidden panel beneath the cart; the screen flashed green text: **“Firmware window 0.7 seconds ready.”** The characters stabbed the dim background like a key entering a rusted lock, the tumbler emitting a faint “click.”  

0.7 seconds passed like a single heartbeat. The nano‑carbon‑tube electrode array inside Subject 7‑0423 completed the write; the unpriced memory of his sister Lin Wei’s sunlit laughter was packaged as a pointer and written to the edge block of the subjects’ public cache. At the instant of writing, his palm scars bled again; droplets slid down his fingers, coalescing into dark‑red spots on the metal floor, each releasing a barely audible “drip” that the corridor’s reverberation amplified into a ritualistic knocking, as if every drop were sealing a contract. The retina flared red: **“Unauthorized write attempt – pending review.”** Vera’s projection ring blazed blue; her fingertip typed “Flag Subject 7‑0423 as high‑risk,” and the screen popped up: **“Approval requires Auditor ‘Zero’ signature, countdown 48 hours.”** The numbers flickered in the air like an hourglass suspended overhead, grains falling one by one into the abyss.  

Cleaning robot 07‑T hesitated, its treads emitting a deeper grinding note than usual; the reluctant meshing of metal on metal resonated through the narrow corridor with an ominous timbre, as though the very air had been drawn taut. Old Chen glanced at the robot’s log; a new entry appeared: **“Environmental interference – physical obstruction.”** It would be auto‑reported to the Central Clearing Core (CCC); the timestamp was precise to the microsecond, pulsing a cold blue. He murmured to Subject 7‑0423, “Machines don’t speak; people must.” His tone was flat, yet the echo gave it weight, each word seeming to add a charge to the impending judgment, the sound refracting off the metal walls in a subtle tremor.  

Subject 7‑0423 did not turn; he only drove his nails deeper into his palm, the sound of blood droplets striking the silence exceptionally clear — the only rhythm he could hear that belonged to him, each drop like a strike on a rusted iron plate. The automatic door at the corridor’s end slid open, a rush of cold air carrying the faint sweet oil scent; the retinal ledger’s crimson flow continued unabated, interest devouring every second without mercy. The ankle cuff remained at a steady 12 °C chill, the mist from the atomizers still hanging in the corridor. He knew the next 0.7‑second window would not arrive for another 30 days, and before then Vera’s review, Zero’s signature, and the ever‑present “Data Circuit Breaker” protocol all ticked down in the countdown. A distant cough from another subject rose again, accompanied by a low laugh that the ductwork stretched into a metallic tremolo: “Don’t think you can run; once the upgrade window closes, even breathing is billed by frequency.” The voice was raspy, permanently hoarse from years of inhaling machine oil and disinfectant, each word striking like a hammer on rusted iron, lingering long in the corridor.  

Subject 7‑0423’s pupils contracted; a new prompt bloomed on his retina: **“Memory correction program initiation countdown 3.2 seconds”** (the forced rewrite automatically launched when the system detects memory integrity FAIL). A whiff of burnt insulation varnish rode the cold air, slipping up his nostrils into his lungs like a rusted rasp dragging across his trachea; each breath brought a faint stab of pain. The intracranial nano‑array released a weak current the moment the countdown lit, crawling along the cranial sutures and delivering a tingling, needle‑like ache as if countless tiny ants were gnawing at his meninges. He clenched his teeth, a low groan escaping his throat; another blood droplet fell, striking the cold floor, its sound swallowed the instant the countdown hit zero, leaving only a faint reverberation trembling on the metal walls.  

Vera’s fingertip hovered over the “Delay Report” key; the blue light of her projection ring refracted in her pupils into two slender blades of light. The red alarm at the corridor’s end snapped on, a shrill siren slicing through the robot’s low growl; the rotors of an external inspection drone whirred close to the metal wall. She did not press confirm; instead she slowly withdrew her hand, the screen’s “Temporary Authorization – pending regulatory review” still pulsing weakly, like a pass that could be torn up at any moment. Old Chen wheeled the cart backward, the axle’s creak fading toward the corridor’s end, leaving only the cleaning robot’s tread growl and the intermittent coughs of distant subjects — an ominous cadence that kept beating against Subject 7‑0423’s heart.
