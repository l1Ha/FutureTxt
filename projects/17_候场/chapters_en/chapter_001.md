# Chapter 1: Viewscore

At 6:10 a.m. on October 30, three hundred and twenty channels would broadcast the skies above Nanyu City.

Release mission briefing. Page one.

Nanyu City was going to fly a Kunpeng across the heavens.

The Kunpeng was drawn. Three hundred meters wingspan, one hundred and seventy thousand light points, spread across the sea's surface. One step remained: fill it in.

Two hundred and seventeen White-shouldered Cranes. Every autumn they came down from the north, rested a night on the Baisha Spit tidal flats, and at dawn the next day turned north again. When they flew scattered, their formation opened wide—like a bird with wings outstretched. Citizens had photographed it for years. In recent years the footage had been cut into the city's promotional reels, into the looping screens at the airport terminal, onto the back covers of foreign investor handbooks.

This year, the promotional reel would become a live broadcast.

On the afternoon of October 19, the Release Command Center summoned Xu Dehai.

The wall screen played the simulated trajectories of the light array. Seventeen thousand light points gathered on the gray-white sea surface, slowly growing into the shape of a bird. Wings spread wide, with several brighter patches within the wings—the anticipated cranes.

"Why would it listen to you?" Xu Dehai asked.

The man across the table pushed his glasses up. His name was Qi Hai, project lead for the Cityscape Platform's Welcome Period landscape and release operations. His shirt cuffs were buttoned at the wrists. He held a pen, turned it half a revolution before speaking.

"The model simulated eight thousand runs," Qi Hai said. "Best day, Match Rate eighty-one percent. For greater certainty, we prepared an alternative."

"What alternative."

"Sound waves first. Directional sound field, herd the flock into the designated airspace."

Xu Dehai watched the glowing bird on the wall. He didn't respond.

He knew sound waves. Scenic areas used them to drive birds away. Parks used them to keep people off the grass. The ancestral method was flags and whistles: sixteen flag signals, two whistle types—one long for descend, two short one long for follow. The flag: one chi eight cun, white cloth with red border.

His father had said once: Birds recognize people, not flags.

But sound waves could push two hundred birds in one direction simultaneously, push them into a shape his father had never achieved in a lifetime.

"How many groups."

"Eighteen."

"Radius."

"Two kilometers."

Xu Dehai fished a four-fold creased paper from his pocket, spread it on the table. His own sketch of the tidal flats: stake beds, tidal channels, reef lines, that abandoned lighthouse.

"Six groups can mount on the stake beds," he said. "The other twelve go in the water, on platforms."

Qi Hai glanced at him, tapped two spots on the paper.

"Master Xu." Qi Hai shifted topics. "One more thing. Your Viewscore: sixty-one."

Xu Dehai didn't look up.

Qi Hai pushed the tablet over. The screen showed a photo of the Old House: three water jars at the doorway, a fishing net drying at the east window, the shed behind the house, blue corrugated steel roof. Below the photo, deduction items listed line by line, each with a code.

"Shed twenty-three square meters, illegal structure. Roof blue tiles not on the color card. Jars facing inward. Flagpole facing east. Fishing net on the window." Qi Hai said, "All minor."

"The camera doesn't see these." Xu Dehai said.

"The camera doesn't see these." Qi Hai repeated. "That's why they deduct your points."

"Who watches."

Qi Hai turned the pen half a revolution. "That I cannot answer."

The viewing corridor matter was the third item. Qi Hai pushed a document over: Code 017, 5:00 to 8:00 a.m. on the 30th, full airspace clearance of Baisha Spit. Boats, net cages, storage piles, self-built sheds—all cleared. Compensation per assessed value.

"Old Tao signed." Qi Hai said. "Boat towed to South District, piles pulled."

Xu Dehai turned to the last page. Compensation: twelve thousand.

"Shed stays." He said.

"Which pile."

"Two piles." Xu Dehai said. "Unit 03 returns every night to the open ground between those two piles. Pull the piles, it lands elsewhere, comes down scattered."

"That's one variable in the model." Qi Hai said.

"That's its roost."

Qi Hai didn't argue. He turned the pen half a revolution. "You keep the piles, release day those two piles appear in frame. Composition-wise, lower left corner."

"I know."

"No compensation either."

"Twelve thousand." Xu Dehai said. "Exactly what my daughter's down payment lacks."

Qi Hai looked at him for a moment.

"Master Xu," he said, "I'm not persuading you. Sign, shed demolished, money arrives, your daughter's side covered. Don't sign, morning of the 30th I won't touch those two piles, frame shows two piles. November 30, hoarding goes up, tidal flats sealed, you can't even enter your overnight roost."

"Can't enter, I'll wait outside."

"Won't wait long." Qi Hai said. "Unit 03 recognizes place, not people."

Xu Dehai pushed the document back.

"Before the 30th, those two piles don't move." He said. "After November 30, you want to demolish, demolish."

He walked out of the command center at 4 p.m. Wind came off the sea, carrying salt and diesel. The command center sat north of the Main Venue, a square building, blue cloth draped at the entrance, a banner printed in English and Chinese: Welcome to the Pan-Pacific Synergy Summit.

He stood outside the building for a while before remembering he hadn't eaten lunch.

His phone vibrated in his trouser pocket. The Cityscape Platform pushed seven recommendations, each with a countdown, lined up on the lock screen.

First: Retract the fishing net at the east window within fifteen minutes. Reason: visual clutter along the viewing corridor.

Second: Three water jars in the courtyard uniformly oriented outward. Reason: façade consistency.

Third: Replace the blue canopy on the tricycle before 8 a.m. tomorrow. Reason: color card non-compliance.

Items four through six addressed neighbors: balcony flower pots not in red terracotta, door mats changed to gray-green, garbage into designated drop points before 10 a.m. daily.

Each recommendation carried a line of fine print: Adoption recorded in Compliance Log, redeemable for service conveniences.

Seventh: Recommend you vacate Baisha Spit before 6 a.m. October 30. Reason: major event security.

Under the seventh, his own reply remained blank.

---

The Old House sat in the city's east, thirty meters above the tidal flats.

Two-story flat-roofed house, blue corrugated steel roof, built by his own hand in 1983. Behind the house adjoined a self-built shed, twenty-three square meters, stacked with fishing nets, bamboo poles, rice sacks, oil lamps, old tires. Two wooden posts stood at the shed's west edge, their tops worn glossy by bird beaks.

Late October sun slanted. Half the courtyard bright, half dark. Three old water jars sat under the east wall—one cracked, one growing aquatic plants, one empty, dry reeds stuck in the hollow jar.

Xu Changgeng sat on the second-floor windowsill, coughing. Handkerchief folded square, pressed to his mouth. When he lowered it, a few black specks remained that wouldn't wash out.

"Back." The old man said.

"Mm."

"What time you rise today."

"Five."

"Watch birds?"

"Studied diagrams."

Xu Changgeng didn't ask further. He pushed the window open a crack. Sea wind rushed in. The old flag on the sill fluttered. White cloth, red border, one chi eight cun. Pole of bamboo, wedged in the brick gap of the sill.

"Unit 03 doesn't count today." The old man said.

Xu Dehai paused. "How do you know."

"Yesterday evening it called four times." The old man said. "Four calls means seeking a place."

---

Master Tao came at dusk. Surname Tao, two years younger than Xu Changgeng. Forty years repairing boats, lived in the iron-sheet shack at the far east of the tidal flats.

He entered coughing twice, stood in the courtyard staring at the two posts.

"Keep my mast for me." He said.

"Keeping."

"Morning of the 30th, camera sweeps past my mast, pans down to your two posts." Master Tao drew a cross in the mud with his toe. "You handle it yourself."

"Understood."

"Old Xu." The old man glanced toward the house, voice dropping. "Twelve thousand. How much you short?"

"Just twelve thousand."

"Then why keep the posts."

"Pull the posts, Unit 03 can't land in its old spot."

Master Tao said nothing more. He fished a paper from his pocket—a photocopy of the compensation agreement, his name and handprint on top.

"I signed." He said. "Mast not pulled either, hidden in the shed, they can't see it." He turned to leave, stopped at the doorway. "Hoarding goes up 30th before dawn. Dogs I'll take first. Those two iron frames less than twenty meters from your posts. You keep distance."

"I don't bring dogs."

"You still bring them." Master Tao said. "My two dogs, you've led them onto the flats all year round."

---

Xu Yi returned at dusk.

She wore a gray-blue vest, number 027 printed on the back, sleeves too long, rolled three times. Entered, washed hands first, then tossed her bag onto a chair.

When she removed the vest, the pink mask's red imprint still marked her nose bridge. Her phone lit up: a line of text from the backend—Today's valid smile duration five hours twelve minutes, qualified.

"Two hundred." She said.

"What two hundred."

"Today two hundred." Xu Yi pulled open the fridge. "Volunteer Smile Citizen, two hundred a day, thirty-six slots, I drew the 27th."

"Where stationed."

"That intersection on Seaside Boulevard. Two p.m. to eight p.m. Issued masks, pink, said must be uniform." She handed him a yogurt. "They have quotas, smiles per minute. Third day I had it down."

Xu Dehai didn't take the yogurt. He watched his daughter's vest, the patch of pink foundation on her nose, a streak washed open by sweat.

"Home early."

"Half past eight." Xu Yi said. "Dad, the down payment—I'm still short twelve thousand."

"I know."

"How you know."

"Today there was an agreement. Twelve thousand. I didn't sign." Xu Dehai said. "The missing twelve thousand, I'll scrape together slowly."

Xu Yi stared at him two seconds. Didn't ask what agreement. She twisted open the yogurt, drank a mouthful.

"What you scraping for." She said. "Your forty-six thousand is what Mom left when she passed. Don't touch it."

"Not touching."

"Then what you scraping with."

Xu Dehai didn't answer. He set the yogurt on the table, went to the kitchen to rinse rice.

Xu Yi followed, leaned against the doorframe.

"Dad," she said. "Résumé missing this one line. Scenic area converting to formal posts next month. I submit it, done."

"Recognized is fine."

"Then why you still worrying."

Xu Dehai rinsed rice twice. Water clouded then cleared. He dumped rice into the pot, water to the second knuckle of his finger. Water sound drowned the rest of her sentence.

"Worry what."

"Worry you." Xu Yi said. "A bird herder, all day wrestling with a flock. Diagram works or not, what's the use."

"Has use."

"What use."

Xu Dehai lit the burner.

"Use is use." He said.

Xu Yi smiled. She smiled like her mother—hummed first.

"Fine." She said. "You tend your birds."

The pot boiled. Steam hit the range hood. The kitchen's faint vegetable scent wouldn't disperse.

---

That night at 10 p.m., the sea wind had risen.

Xu Dehai exited the back door, circled the self-built shed, walked to the tidal flat entrance.

The two-meter gap at the entrance—his father had cobbled it from two ship planks years ago, roofed with oil felt. Shed couldn't be demolished, this gap couldn't be sealed. Seal it, Unit 03 couldn't return.

Movement already on the flats. Around the stake beds, White-shouldered Cranes squatted in a mass, gray-white bodies pressed together, feet in water. From afar a stretch of gray-white dots. Closer, each one moving: heads down pecking, heads up shifting feet, a wing shudder.

Xu Dehai stepped through mud toward the east. Walk a stretch, stop, glance.

Unit 03 stood at the far east of the flats, two hundred meters from the main flock.

It stood on one leg, the other tucked up, neck retracted into shoulders, left wing held lower than the right by a notch. Surrounding cranes scattered. It didn't crowd in, didn't wander far.

Xu Dehai watched a long time before understanding what it was doing.

It was waiting. Waiting for the main flock to approach, then leave together.

On his way back, the stake bed area had new additions.

Six tripods, steel, each topped with a horn-mouthed box. Weight blocks at the base, cables running from the flats toward shore. Two more identical tripods at the stake bed edge, several other groups still being assembled in the water.

Workers held lamps, light swaying in wind. Someone drilled bolts on weight blocks, the bite of drill on steel carrying far across the flats.

He counted.

Eighteen groups.

Horn mouths pointed skyward. He walked over, pressed his palm to the nearest horn. Metal cold.

Tomorrow 3:40 a.m., he had to clear the flats. 4:20, first group activates.

Whether two hundred birds would move together in the direction he indicated—he didn't know. He'd coaxed a flock in his life, coaxed a single bird; coaxing flock and bird together, never once.

Wind came off the sea. He slipped his hand into his pocket, felt the four-fold creased paper. A dry pen at the paper's edge.
